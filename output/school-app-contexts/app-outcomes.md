# Pedagogical outcomes and lesson placement

Companion files: [context gallery](index.html), [context teaching notes](teaching-notes.md) and [screen index](contexts.csv).

Each app needs a clear learning purpose and an observable result: something the student can explain, demonstrate or apply afterwards. These are proposed outcomes and lesson uses based on the app concepts. They are design intentions, rather than demonstrated effects or a formal mapping to curriculum content codes.

Teachers introduce the relevant concepts, use the app for a focused task, then check whether students can apply the learning beyond that task. Timings are indicative. The assessment examples describe evidence to collect, not universal mastery thresholds. Record the support a student received.

The selected screenshots each illustrate one task within the broader app purpose. Teachers choose content and scaffolding for the student’s year level and current understanding. Drawing, oral responses, accessible controls and teacher-supported prompts can reduce barriers without changing the learning goal; when written composition or decoding is itself the goal, the assessment still needs evidence of that specific skill.

## 1. Classroom Words — Foundation–Year 2

The central outcome is **more accurate, increasingly independent decoding using the sound–letter relationships already taught in class**. Its value is the match between classroom instruction and student practice.

Students work towards being able to:

- **Recognise taught sound–letter relationships:** connect a written letter or letter group with its corresponding sound.
- **Blend sounds to read words:** move from identifying individual sounds to combining them into a spoken word.
- **Segment words for spelling:** identify the sounds in a spoken word and select the corresponding taught letters.
- **Read connected text:** apply decoding to short sentences, then reread with increasing accuracy and appropriate phrasing.
- **Monitor meaning:** notice when a decoded word does not make sense, revisit it and explain the meaning of a short passage.

These aims connect to the English curriculum’s emphasis on sound–letter correspondences and their application in reading and writing. [Australian Curriculum: English](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/curriculum-information/understand-this-learning-area/english)

**Lesson placement:** guided practice immediately after explicit phonics teaching, a short literacy rotation or targeted consolidation with a teacher.

**Example: a 30-minute Foundation phonics lesson**

1. **Teach and model, 7 minutes:** review the selected sounds and demonstrate blending using physical letter cards.
2. **Practise together, 5 minutes:** build and read words containing only those taught correspondences.
3. **App practice, 10 minutes:** complete word-building activities and read a short decodable passage. The teacher listens to selected students.
4. **Transfer, 8 minutes:** read unfamiliar words with the same correspondences from paper, then write a dictated word or sentence.

**Evidence:** accurate reading of new words, fewer prompts, successful blending and a brief explanation of the passage’s meaning. Game completion alone provides limited evidence.

**Progression:** begin with a small set of correspondences and simple word structures; increase complexity according to the teaching sequence and demonstrated readiness. Keep rich read-alouds, vocabulary teaching and discussion alongside this focused decoding practice.

## 2. Think in Parts — Years 5–9

The central outcome is **a coherent explanation of how or why something works**. Drawing, animation and voice give students ways to make relationships visible.

Students work towards being able to:

- **Break a concept into meaningful components:** identify the parts, stages or factors that matter.
- **Explain relationships:** show how one part affects another, distinguishing a description from a causal explanation.
- **Choose an appropriate representation:** use an arrow for movement, a sequence for change over time or a diagram for structure.
- **Coordinate representations:** ensure narration, labels and animation communicate a consistent explanation.
- **Revise understanding:** identify a missing link or contradiction and improve the explanation after feedback.

**Lesson placement:** an initial diagnostic task, consolidation after an investigation or an assessed explanation near the end of a unit.

**Example: a 50-minute Year 7 science lesson on particle models**

1. **Retrieve, 5 minutes:** explain what students already think happens when a substance changes state.
2. **Model, 10 minutes:** compare a labelled picture with an explanation connecting particle arrangement and movement.
3. **Create, 20 minutes:** construct a short narrated explanation of one change of state.
4. **Review, 10 minutes:** partners identify one clear relationship and one point needing explanation.
5. **Transfer, 5 minutes:** explain a related example without using the finished presentation.

**Evidence:** conceptual accuracy, connected reasoning and the ability to answer a follow-up question. Assess visual polish separately so sophisticated animation does not obscure weak understanding.

**Progression:** less experienced students complete a partially prepared diagram. Older students compare competing models, explain exceptions or state where a model stops being useful. The same structure can support a mathematical explanation or a historical cause-and-consequence task.

## 3. Town Together — Years 5–9

The central outcome is **a defensible public decision that accounts for evidence, competing needs and limited resources**.

Students work towards being able to:

- **Identify the public issue:** explain what needs deciding and which institution has responsibility.
- **Interpret stakeholder perspectives:** distinguish a preference from the interests or circumstances behind it.
- **Evaluate proposals against criteria:** consider cost, access, likely benefit, environmental effects and fairness.
- **Explain trade-offs:** identify what becomes possible, what is forgone and who may be disadvantaged.
- **Participate in deliberation:** question proposals, respond to objections and revise a position when evidence warrants it.

This fits the HASS emphasis on inquiry, informed decisions and civic participation. [Australian Curriculum: Humanities and Social Sciences](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/curriculum-information/understand-this-learning-area/humanities-and-social-sciences)

**Lesson placement:** the application stage of a civics unit, after teaching the relevant decision-making body.

**Example: two 50-minute Year 6 lessons on improving a community space**

1. **Establish the context:** teach the relevant local government responsibilities and examine the site.
2. **Investigate:** groups hear stakeholder views, inspect proposals and identify missing information.
3. **Deliberate:** apply agreed criteria and prepare a recommendation within the budget.
4. **Hold a council meeting:** present, question and amend proposals.
5. **Reflect individually:** explain a preferred option, its main drawback and the strongest argument against it.

**Evidence:** a recommendation supported by evidence, accurate budget reasoning and an individual explanation of the trade-off. Winning the vote is not evidence of stronger reasoning.

**Progression:** Years 5–6 compare two proposals with a simple budget. Years 7–9 examine source reliability, recurring costs, unequal impacts and consultation processes. Council and parliamentary scenarios must teach their different powers and procedures explicitly.

## 4. Wild Notebook — Years 5–9

The central outcome is **a supported scientific explanation drawn from systematic observation of a real place**.

Students work towards being able to:

- **Ask an investigable question:** turn broad curiosity into something observable or measurable.
- **Record consistently:** use agreed categories, units, locations and sampling procedures.
- **Separate observation from inference:** distinguish a count from an explanation for it.
- **Compare data:** identify patterns between locations or across time.
- **Evaluate uncertainty:** recognise incomplete identification, uneven sampling and the limits of a small dataset.

**Lesson placement:** fieldwork preparation, outdoor data collection and subsequent discussion, or a repeated investigation across a term.

**Example: two Year 5 lessons comparing school-ground habitats**

1. **Prepare:** ask, “What differences do we observe between a garden bed and a paved edge?” Agree on observation time, sampling area and recording categories.
2. **Investigate outdoors:** observe assigned locations and record counts, sketches, photographs and environmental conditions.
3. **Compare in class:** combine findings and identify similarities and differences.
4. **Explain:** propose an explanation and identify another observation that would help test it.

**Evidence:** records another group can interpret, comparisons using actual data and conclusions limited to those observations. “We found more organisms in these garden samples” is more defensible than “Gardens always have more biodiversity.”

**Progression:** younger students use teacher-defined categories and tables. Older students design sampling methods, compare repeated samples and assess alternative explanations. Weather investigations need repeated observations over time. A large organism count alone does not establish greater biodiversity; distinguish abundance, the variety of groups identified and uncertainty in identification.

## 5. TinkerTest — Years 5–9

The central outcome is **designing and interpreting an investigation**, including understanding what a simulation can and cannot establish.

Students work towards being able to:

- **Make a reasoned prediction:** state an expected relationship and explain the reasoning.
- **Identify variables:** specify what changes, what is measured and what is held constant.
- **Plan an informative test:** choose useful values and a procedure addressing the question.
- **Interpret results:** connect tables or graphs to the original prediction.
- **Evaluate the model:** distinguish simulated results from physical measurements and identify assumptions.

Wild Notebook and TinkerTest offer complementary experiences: field investigation and controlled simulation. Both are recognised forms of science inquiry, but answer questions in different ways. [Australian Curriculum: Science](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/curriculum-information/understand-this-learning-area/science)

**Lesson placement:** investigation rehearsal before practical work, exploration when equipment is unavailable or extension after practical work.

**Example: a 50-minute Year 8 pendulum investigation**

1. **Predict, 5 minutes:** predict how string length affects the time for a complete swing.
2. **Plan, 10 minutes:** identify variables and agree on useful lengths to compare.
3. **Test, 15 minutes:** run the simulation and record results systematically.
4. **Interpret, 10 minutes:** represent the relationship and compare it with the prediction.
5. **Evaluate, 10 minutes:** explain what a physical investigation adds, including measurement variation.

**Evidence:** an appropriate plan, correctly interpreted results and an evidence-based conclusion. A prediction that turns out to be wrong can still demonstrate productive reasoning.

**Progression:** provide structured investigations for novices and gradually remove prompts. Where a simulation produces identical results on every run, repeated clicks must not be presented as evidence about real-world reliability.

## 6. Number Kitchen — Years 1–4

The central outcome is **using mathematical relationships to solve a practical quantity problem and explain the solution**.

Students work towards being able to:

- **Connect quantities and representations:** relate objects, pictures, spoken number language and calculations.
- **Use grouping and sharing:** form equal groups, distribute fairly and explain remainders where appropriate.
- **Understand fractions:** identify the whole and explain why parts must be equal.
- **Measure purposefully:** select units, read scales and compare estimated and measured quantities.
- **Check reasonableness:** decide whether a quantity, cost or answer makes sense in context.

This supports understanding, fluency, reasoning and problem-solving. [Australian Curriculum: Mathematics](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/curriculum-information/understand-this-learning-area/mathematics)

**Lesson placement:** guided application after explicit teaching, a mathematics rotation or a bridge between physical materials and symbolic work.

**Example: a 45-minute Year 2 lesson on doubling**

1. **Build, 10 minutes:** represent a recipe for two people with counters or model food.
2. **Model, 5 minutes:** show how making it for four people changes every ingredient.
3. **Apply, 15 minutes:** adjust a virtual recipe and explain quantities to a partner.
4. **Represent, 10 minutes:** draw or write the calculation behind the changes.
5. **Transfer, 5 minutes:** solve a doubling problem in a different context.

**Evidence:** correct quantities across all ingredients and an explanation of the relationship. Correct answers reached by repeated guessing need a follow-up check.

**Progression:** Years 1–2 focus on counting, equal groups, simple sharing and comparison. Years 3–4 explore fractions, scales and money at an appropriate level. Select one main mathematical objective per lesson.

## 7. StoryChirps — Foundation–Year 3

The central outcome is **an increasingly coherent spoken account that a listener can follow**.

Students work towards being able to:

- **Sequence events:** establish a meaningful order and communicate it through language.
- **Connect ideas:** use words such as “first”, “after”, “because” and “finally” appropriately.
- **Add useful detail:** identify characters, settings and actions without relying on the listener seeing the pictures.
- **Adapt to an audience:** include needed information and make references clear.
- **Listen and revise:** identify confusion or missing information in a recording and improve it.

**Lesson placement:** oral-language groups, preparation for writing, responses to shared texts or short formative assessments.

**Example: a 30-minute Year 1 storytelling lesson**

1. **Model, 5 minutes:** tell a story from three pictures, leaving out an important connection.
2. **Discuss, 5 minutes:** identify what the listener needs to know.
3. **Rehearse and record, 10 minutes:** arrange pictures, tell a story and record it.
4. **Listen and improve, 5 minutes:** make one purposeful change.
5. **Share, 5 minutes:** compare different stories built from the same images.

**Evidence:** clearer order, precise references and a revision that helps the listener understand. Preserve early and later recordings for comparison against an explicit language focus.

**Progression and access:** move from short picture-supported utterances to connected narratives, explanations and audience-aware retellings. EAL/D learners can rehearse using home language, gesture or a partner before developing the English version. Assess English development separately from conceptual understanding; proficiency can differ across speaking, listening, reading and writing. [ACARA: EAL/D students](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/student-diversity/eal-d-students)

## 8. Plot a Place — Years 1–6

The central outcome is **using spatial representations to communicate where things are and explain how a place serves people’s needs**.

Students work towards being able to:

- **Represent a place from above:** translate lived experience into a plan or map.
- **Use symbols consistently:** create and interpret a key.
- **Communicate location and direction:** describe routes with increasingly precise spatial language.
- **Apply scale appropriately:** connect map distances and real distances when ready.
- **Reason about place:** explain how paths, facilities and landmarks affect different users.

**Lesson placement:** after a local walk, within a mapping sequence or as a design-and-justify task.

**Example: a 50-minute Year 3 classroom-mapping lesson**

1. **Observe, 10 minutes:** discuss what would be visible from above.
2. **Model, 10 minutes:** convert a small area into a plan with symbols and a key.
3. **Construct, 15 minutes:** build maps in the app.
4. **Test, 10 minutes:** partners follow a route described using the map.
5. **Revise, 5 minutes:** correct an unclear symbol, position or instruction.

**Evidence:** another student can interpret the map and reach the destination. The mapmaker can explain why a feature belongs in its location.

**Progression:** Years 1–2 use familiar places, relative position and pictorial symbols. Years 3–4 develop compass directions and simple grids. Years 5–6 use measured scale and compare accessibility or land-use decisions. Follow an imaginary-island task with a school-ground task to check transfer.

## 9. Clue Canopy — Years 2–6

The central outcome is **a reading interpretation supported by relevant textual evidence**.

Students work towards being able to:

- **Distinguish explicit information from inference:** identify what is stated and what they are working out.
- **Select relevant evidence:** choose a detail that supports the particular claim.
- **Explain the connection:** articulate how the clue leads to the interpretation.
- **Consider alternatives:** recognise when more than one explanation remains plausible.
- **Update an interpretation:** change a prediction or inference when new evidence appears.

**Lesson placement:** guided reading, comprehension mini-lessons, paired discussion or diagnostic reasoning checks.

**Example: a 40-minute Year 4 inference lesson**

1. **Model, 8 minutes:** think aloud about an inference and identify supporting words.
2. **Read and investigate, 12 minutes:** examine a short mystery and select clues.
3. **Explain, 10 minutes:** use speech, drawing or writing to explain “I think … because the text says …”.
4. **Reconsider, 5 minutes:** reveal a passage that complicates the initial interpretation.
5. **Transfer, 5 minutes:** infer something from a new text without the app’s prompts.

**Evidence:** a plausible claim, relevant evidence and an explicit connection. Guessing the ending is less informative than explaining the reasoning.

**Progression:** begin with one inference supported by nearby details; progress to combining clues, resolving contradictions and considering narrator reliability. A teacher read-aloud can separate comprehension from decoding difficulty; record that support. Illustrations should establish context without supplying the answer to a textual inference task.

## 10. SwitchSide Stories — Foundation–Year 4

The central outcome is **considering another person’s perspective and explaining possible responses to a social situation**.

Position primarily within **Health and Physical Education and personal and social capability**, with connections to English and HASS. ACARA identifies perspective-taking, communication, collaboration and conflict resolution within personal and social capability. [Australian Curriculum: Personal and Social Capability](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/curriculum-information/understand-this-general-capability/personal-and-social-capability)

Students work towards being able to:

- **Describe possible feelings and needs:** use tentative language when interpreting another person.
- **Recognise different perspectives:** explain how the same event can be experienced differently.
- **Generate responses:** suggest more than one constructive action.
- **Anticipate consequences:** consider how a response affects each person.
- **Practise communication and help-seeking:** ask, clarify, negotiate or seek adult support when needed.

**Lesson placement:** teacher-led discussion, a planned relationships lesson or preparation for cooperative work.

**Example: a 25-minute Year 2 lesson about sharing materials**

1. **Introduce, 5 minutes:** view a fictional disagreement without deciding who is right.
2. **Explore, 5 minutes:** consider what each character might want and what remains unknown.
3. **Compare, 7 minutes:** discuss possible responses and consequences.
4. **Rehearse, 5 minutes:** role-play asking, negotiating or requesting help.
5. **Reflect, 3 minutes:** name a response and explain when it might help.

**Evidence:** acknowledging multiple perspectives, suggesting a workable response and explaining its likely effect. Do not score personality, empathy or a preferred communication style.

**Progression:** younger students work with concrete needs and short exchanges. Older students consider competing responsibilities, intentions and unintended consequences. Preserve several reasonable responses while making boundaries around harm and safety clear.

## 11. Instruction Impossible — Years 2–6

The central outcome is **writing sufficiently precise instructions for someone else to carry out a task successfully**.

Students work towards being able to:

- **Sequence necessary actions:** include steps in an order that makes the task possible.
- **Use precise language:** specify action, object, direction, quantity or stopping point.
- **Anticipate reader knowledge:** identify information the writer has assumed.
- **Diagnose ambiguity:** connect an unexpected result to a missing or unclear instruction.
- **Revise and retest:** make a purposeful change and check whether it resolves the problem.

**Lesson placement:** procedural writing, preparation for a science method or a connection between English and algorithmic thinking.

**Example: a 45-minute Year 3 lesson on clear directions**

1. **Demonstrate, 5 minutes:** follow a deliberately vague instruction literally.
2. **Teach, 10 minutes:** identify features of usable instructions and improve one together.
3. **Write and test, 15 minutes:** direct a character through a short challenge.
4. **Revise, 10 minutes:** explain a failure, change wording and exchange instructions.
5. **Transfer, 5 minutes:** partners follow written instructions for arranging physical objects.

**Evidence:** successful execution by a new reader, fewer missing steps and an explanation of why a revision helped. The wording matters as much as reaching the destination.

**Progression:** Years 2–3 focus on ordered actions and spatial vocabulary. Years 4–6 add precise conditions, repetition and exceptions where suitable. When writing is the objective, students compose language; selecting prepared command blocks alone provides limited evidence of writing development.

## 12. Edit Echidna — Years 2–6

The central outcome is **making and explaining a purposeful revision that improves a text for its reader**.

Students work towards being able to:

- **Understand a revision goal:** recognise clearer sequencing, stronger detail or better support.
- **Locate a weakness:** identify a part of their own writing needing attention.
- **Choose an effective change:** add, remove, reorder or replace material for a reason.
- **Compare versions:** explain how the revision changes the reader’s understanding.
- **Transfer the strategy:** apply it to another piece with less prompting.

**Lesson placement:** the revision stage of a writing workshop, immediately after feedback or in a short teacher conference.

**Example: a 40-minute Year 5 lesson on supporting a persuasive claim**

1. **Model, 8 minutes:** compare an unsupported claim with one developed through relevant evidence and explanation.
2. **Locate, 7 minutes:** select a claim in the student’s own draft that needs support.
3. **Revise, 15 minutes:** improve the passage and compare versions.
4. **Explain, 5 minutes:** write or record why the change helps the reader.
5. **Apply again, 5 minutes:** identify another passage where the same strategy would help.

**Evidence:** an improvement tied to the goal and a credible explanation of its effect. More words, longer sentences or more adjectives do not automatically indicate better writing.

**Progression:** Years 2–3 focus on event order, clear references and useful detail. Years 4–6 work on paragraph organisation, cohesion, evidence and audience. Keep revision of meaning distinct from proofreading. A sequencing revision must preserve the author’s intended events; the app must not silently reverse what happened.
